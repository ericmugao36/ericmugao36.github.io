Imagine it is election season in Kenya. Your friend is campaigning for governor. Her posters are everywhere. Her supporters are singing her praises, promising a new beginning. The family is quietly preparing for the possibilities that come with victory.

Then, one evening, her mother appears on television.

She looks into the camera and says: “My daughter is lying to you. Do not elect her.”

What happens next?

I suspect the country would stop talking about almost everything else.

That thought came to me after watching a controversial political advertisement from Ohio, in which Vivek Ramaswamy’s campaign featured the estranged mother of his opponent, Dr. Amy Acton, accusing her daughter of lying about her childhood. 

In an campaign ad for her daughter's opponent the mother says, "I love my mother but when I saw her lying about her opponent, it's not easy for a mother to say this, but Amy has a serious problem. She is a liar." She continues, "She can't help you if she needs help herself."

It took a few seconds to acknowledge that this is one of the best ads I have watched.

Still, one question stayed with me, how many relatives would dare do this in Kenya?

We are accustomed to family members standing behind political candidates. A brother endorses a brother. A cousin mobilises supporters. A parent prays for a child’s victory. Family loyalty becomes part of the campaign machinery, because the anticipated benefits of political success extend far beyond the candidate.

Imagine, then, a mother refusing to play her expected role. Imagine a brother saying, “I love my sister, but what she is telling the public is not true.” Imagine a cousin risking family relationships to expose a dishonest promise. Such moments would be uncomfortable, perhaps even devastating. 

But they might also force us to confront a question we rarely ask: when did protecting our own become more important than protecting the truth?

Of course, a mother is not automatically right because she is a mother. Family disputes can be bitter, and relatives can lie, manipulate or seek revenge. We must examine the evidence, the history and the motives behind the accusation.

**That lesson Kenya needs.**

When we analyse politics in Kenya, we discover that the problem goes far beyond relatives standing behind their own. We have built a political culture in which even our neighbours become our campaign agents. If a presidential candidate comes from your tribe, someone next door may remind you why you must vote for that person simply because they are one of your own. This can happen even when you have evidence that the candidate is dishonest, or when you believe there is someone with better ideas who could lead the country more effectively.

We have abandoned ideas for identity, and truth for personal benefit.

Too often, we support people not because of what they stand for, but because of who they are and what we hope to gain from their victory. We imagine that when someone we know gets into power, we too will somehow benefit. We forget that leadership is not a family inheritance or a tribal reward; it is a responsibility entrusted to someone on behalf of an entire nation.

Yet, if we allowed the best ideas to win, everyone would stand to benefit—even those who might otherwise have hoped to exploit the system for personal gain. Imagine a country where public resources are managed honestly, opportunities are awarded fairly, and institutions work as they should. Even the person who might have benefited from a corrupt arrangement would be safer living in a country where the rules protect everyone. They would not have to spend their life wondering whether someone else was coming to steal what they had worked for.

We should not wait for a politician’s mother to expose the truth. We must build a culture in which parents, siblings, cousins, friends and neighbours can challenge dishonesty without being treated as traitors. More importantly, we must learn to listen, investigate and hold leaders accountable, even when the truth threatens someone we love, someone from our community, or someone whose victory we hope will benefit us.

Because integrity is not proved when speaking the truth is convenient. It is proven when silence would be easier, when loyalty would be rewarded, and when telling the truth could cost someone a powerful position.

Perhaps the real question is not whether a mother would publicly call her daughter a liar while she campaigns to become governor.

Perhaps it is this: If we cannot tell the truth about our own, how can we expect our leaders to tell the truth about our country?
