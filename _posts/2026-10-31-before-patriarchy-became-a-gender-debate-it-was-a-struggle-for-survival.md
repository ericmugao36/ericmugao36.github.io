There is a tendency in modern discussions about patriarchy to frame its entire existence around one proposition: men invented a system to dominate women.

But is that really the whole story?

To understand patriarchy, we must first understand the world in which it developed. We must look beyond modern social arrangements and examine the conditions under which human societies organized themselves, protected their resources, established authority and ensured their survival.

For much of human history, the greatest threats to a man's property, territory, wealth, political authority and even his life often came from other men.

Men fought over land. They fought over livestock, water, food, trade routes, political positions and control of communities. They formed alliances, waged wars, conquered territories and competed for resources. The outcome of these conflicts determined who possessed wealth, who exercised authority, who controlled territory and whose descendants would inherit the advantages.

This was a world in which there were no modern police forces to respond to threats, no reliable emergency services, no established system of universal legal protection and, in many communities, no central authority powerful enough to guarantee the security of individuals and their property.

Survival depended heavily on family structures, alliances, physical defence, community organisation and the ability to protect what one possessed.

And much of that responsibility fell on men.

It is within this historical context that we should examine the development of patriarchal institutions.

The argument that patriarchy was exclusively an invention for the oppression of women ignores an important part of the picture: **patriarchy also organized competition, authority and relationships between men.**

## The first question was not simply who controlled women, but who controlled resources

Imagine living in a society where your family's survival depends on a piece of land, a herd of cattle, access to water or the ability to protect your harvest.

There is no police station to call when another group attempts to take your property. There is no modern court system to resolve every dispute. There may be no government institution capable of defending your family against an organized attack.

Your ability to retain what you possess depends on your family, your alliances, your community and your capacity to defend your interests.

Now consider the fact that other men are competing for the same resources.

They may want your land, your livestock, your position of influence or the wealth your family has accumulated over generations.

This is not a theoretical conflict between men and women. It is a material struggle between individuals, families and communities, frequently involving men on both sides.

Early societies had to establish arrangements for determining ownership, authority, inheritance, family obligations and the consequences of violating established social rules.

Patriarchal structures became part of how many societies organized these relationships.

They helped establish who headed a household, who represented a family, who inherited property, who was responsible for defending family interests and how authority was transferred between generations.

These were not insignificant arrangements. They determined the distribution of resources, the stability of families and the balance of power within communities.

##  Patriarchy was also about controlling competition between men

One of the biggest omissions in contemporary discussions about patriarchy is the assumption that men operated as one united group with common interests.

They did not.

A king and a peasant were both men, but their experiences were worlds apart. A wealthy landowner and a landless labourer did not enjoy the same privileges. A powerful family could determine the future of its descendants, while an ordinary man might spend his entire life struggling to acquire enough resources to support his household.

Men competed against one another for wealth, land, political influence, social status and inheritance.

Some men accumulated enormous power. Others were expected to serve, work, fight, pay taxes, provide labour or defend the interests of those above them.

In many historical societies, powerful men established institutions that protected their positions and determined the conditions under which other men could access resources and authority.

This is an important distinction.

When we say that patriarchy concentrated authority in men, we should also ask: Which men? Under what circumstances? And how was that authority distributed?

A wealthy man's son could inherit land, livestock, political connections and social standing simply by virtue of his family background. A poor man's son could inherit little beyond the economic circumstances into which he was born.

Both belonged to the same gender, but they did not occupy the same position in the social hierarchy.

Patriarchy was therefore not a system in which every man enjoyed equal power. It was also a system of hierarchy among men, with authority concentrated in particular families, classes and political institutions.

The ordinary man was not necessarily the architect of the system. In many cases, he was also subject to its demands.

## Property, inheritance and the importance of family lineage

To understand why patriarchal societies placed so much emphasis on marriage, paternity and family lineage, we must understand the importance of inheritance.

Property was not simply about personal comfort. It was about security, influence, economic survival and the ability to maintain a family's position across generations.

If a family had accumulated land, livestock, wealth or political influence, it had an interest in ensuring that these assets remained within its recognized lineage.

In societies organized around male inheritance, sons were generally expected to carry the family name and inherit the family's property.

This made questions of paternity and legitimate heirs particularly important.

Marriage became an institution through which families established recognized relationships, formed alliances and organized the transfer of wealth between generations.

Women's reproductive roles consequently became closely regulated in many patriarchal societies. Rules governing marriage, fidelity and sexual behaviour were connected to questions of inheritance and the certainty of family lineage.

This is one reason why control over women's sexuality became such a prominent feature of patriarchal arrangements.

It was not simply a matter of men exercising authority for its own sake. In many societies, it was connected to the organisation of property, inheritance and family continuity.

Of course, these arrangements also gave men considerable control over women's personal lives. But understanding the economic and institutional interests behind them gives us a fuller explanation of why they developed and why they persisted.

## Authority came with obligations

Another aspect that is frequently overlooked is the relationship between authority and responsibility.

In many traditional societies, men were expected to provide for their households, defend family property, participate in warfare, protect their communities and assume responsibility for difficult physical tasks.

These expectations were not necessarily optional. They were part of the social obligations associated with being a man.

Men were often the ones expected to confront external threats, participate in territorial defence and risk their lives during conflicts between communities.

The authority they exercised within households and communities was closely connected, in many societies, to these expectations.

Women and children were often placed under male protection and provision. In return, men were expected to fulfil responsibilities associated with defending and sustaining the household.

This arrangement created a relationship between protection, provision, authority and obligation.

It also created inequalities. Women could have limited control over property, economic independence and personal decisions, even while contributing substantially to the survival and prosperity of their families.

But we cannot examine the authority without examining the obligations that accompanied it, just as we cannot examine the privileges of powerful men without examining the burdens placed on ordinary men.

A historical system should be examined in its entirety, not selectively.

## The protection of women and children was also connected to the protection of family interests

In societies where violence, raids and warfare were recurring threats, protecting one's family was inseparable from protecting one's resources and future.

A family was not simply a collection of individuals living together. It was an economic unit, a social institution, a source of labour, a political alliance and a mechanism for transferring resources between generations.

The loss of family members, the destruction of property or the displacement of an entire household could threaten the survival of a lineage.

Consequently, protecting women and children was also connected to protecting the continuity of the family.

This does not mean that women were treated equally within these arrangements. In many cases, their protection was accompanied by restrictions on their autonomy and independence.

But it challenges the simplistic assumption that the historical relationship between men and women can only be understood through the desire to dominate.

Protection, economic interdependence, family continuity, social obligations and authority all formed part of the relationship.

These considerations could coexist with inequality.



## Understanding patriarchy requires looking beyond gender alone

None of this means that women did not experience discrimination under patriarchal systems. They certainly did.

Many were denied property rights, excluded from political decision-making, restricted in their economic opportunities and subjected to rules that gave male relatives considerable control over their lives.

Those realities deserve examination.

But the existence of these inequalities does not explain the entirety of patriarchy's historical development.

If we only examine patriarchy through the relationship between men and women, we miss the competition between men, the struggles over property, the concentration of wealth, the organisation of inheritance, the demands of warfare and the establishment of political authority.

We also risk treating men as though they were a single class of beneficiaries, when history demonstrates that power was distributed very unevenly among them.

The powerful man and the ordinary man did not experience patriarchy in the same way. One could inherit an empire; another could die defending someone else's territory. One could make laws; another was expected to obey them.

The fact that both were men did not erase those differences.

**The point is not that patriarchy had nothing to do with the subordination of women. The point is that explaining its entire history through that single relationship is an incomplete analysis.**

Patriarchy was also about establishing authority, regulating competition, protecting property, organising inheritance, maintaining family structures and determining how power was distributed across generations.

And much of the struggle over those things was between men themselves.

If we want an honest discussion about patriarchy, we must be willing to examine all these dimensions, including the uncomfortable reality that men have historically been both beneficiaries and victims of the hierarchies established by patriarchal institutions.

We should be able to distinguish between the men who designed and controlled institutions, the men who benefited from them, and the men who were expected to fight, labour and sacrifice within them.

History is rarely explained by a single motive. And patriarchy is no exception.

**Perhaps the more useful question is not simply why men created patriarchy to exercise power over women, but why human societies developed systems that organized power, property, protection, inheritance and authority in the way they did — and why those systems placed different expectations and restrictions on men and women alike.**
