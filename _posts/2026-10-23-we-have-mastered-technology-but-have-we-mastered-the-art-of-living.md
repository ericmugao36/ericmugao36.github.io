***On the loss of patience, the purpose of education, and a civilization that has forgotten how to build for centuries.***

There is something strange about being human in the modern world. We live in an age of extraordinary technological advancement. We can communicate across continents in seconds, access more information than any generation before us, automate complicated tasks, and build machines that would have seemed like magic to our ancestors. We have become remarkably good at making things happen faster.

Yet I sometimes wonder whether, in our pursuit of efficiency, we have forgotten how to make things meaningful.

We have mastered many technologies, but have we mastered the art of living? Do we still know how to build something beautiful, learn something deeply, or pursue an idea without immediately asking what it will earn us? Have we become so occupied with the demands of today that we have lost the ability to imagine what the world might need a hundred years from now?

## The Beauty of Things That Outlive Their Creators

Consider the great cathedrals of Europe. Some took generations to complete. The people who laid their foundations often knew they would never see the finished buildings. They worked on structures whose completion belonged to people they would never meet.

Think about the extraordinary commitment behind that idea. A stonemason might spend years perfecting a craft. An architect might develop a design that would require decades of labour. A community might contribute resources to a building that would continue standing long after its original members were gone.

The result was not merely a building. It was a statement about time, belief, community, craftsmanship, and the human desire to create something greater than oneself.

We see similar ambition in art. Michelangelo's *David*, sculpted between 1501 and 1504, is one of the most celebrated statues in history. The block of marble had already been worked on by other sculptors before Michelangelo undertook the challenge. What emerged was a work of extraordinary technical skill and artistic vision.

The statue reminds us that creating something remarkable requires more than access to materials or tools. It requires patience, judgement, imagination, and a willingness to devote oneself to a difficult task.

Of course, the past was not a paradise of noble craftsmanship. Many monumental buildings depended on the labour of ordinary people whose names were never recorded, and some were built under harsh conditions. History contains its share of exploitation, vanity, and failed ambitions.

But the surviving masterpieces raise an important question: what kind of society makes room for work whose value cannot be measured by its immediate return?

This question matters just as much in Africa as it does in Europe. We have our own architectural histories, artistic traditions, systems of knowledge, and forms of craftsmanship. Across the continent, people developed ways of building and living suited to their climates, materials, communities, and beliefs. These traditions deserve to be studied and developed, not treated as obstacles to modernity.

Modern Africa does not have to imitate European cathedrals to demonstrate ambition. Our ambition might take the form of beautiful civic buildings, well-designed schools, public libraries, enduring infrastructure, or neighbourhoods that reflect our own histories and aspirations.

The important question is whether we are building places that express what we value, or simply places that satisfy the immediate demands of commerce.

## When Everything Must Justify Its Existence

Modern society is organised around productivity. We measure growth, output, revenue, engagement, efficiency, and performance. Businesses are expected to expand. Workers are expected to remain productive. Institutions are expected to demonstrate measurable results. Even our personal lives increasingly resemble projects that require optimisation.

There is nothing inherently wrong with efficiency or profit. Businesses need to survive, workers need to earn a living, and societies need functioning economies. Technology has made it possible to improve health, education, communication, and living standards in ways earlier generations could only imagine.

The problem begins when these things become the only measures of value.

What happens when a beautiful public space is considered less important than the money that could be made from the land? What happens when a school is judged almost entirely by examination results? What happens when a person chooses a career solely by its earning potential, even when the work offers no deeper sense of purpose?

We begin to treat everything as an instrument: education becomes a route to employment, art becomes content, relationships become networks, and time becomes a resource that must never be wasted.

Capitalism is particularly effective at turning attention, labour, and creativity into economic value. That ability has generated enormous innovation. Yet markets do not automatically reward everything that makes a civilisation worth living in. They may reward what is profitable now while neglecting what would benefit people over decades or centuries.

A tree that takes fifty years to mature is a difficult proposition for someone concerned only with next quarter's returns. A public library may enrich an entire community without producing the kind of financial return that makes an investor excited. A school that teaches curiosity, patience, and independent thought may produce benefits that are difficult to capture in a spreadsheet.

Consider the way we sometimes talk about land in our cities. Its value is frequently discussed in terms of what can be constructed on it, what it can be sold for, or how much rent it can generate. But a public space also has value because children can play there, neighbours can meet, older people can rest, and communities can develop a shared sense of belonging.

Research on Nairobi's public spaces has highlighted challenges involving accessibility, quality, distribution, and maintenance. These are not merely questions of urban planning. They are questions about the kind of life a city makes possible for its residents.

A city is not successful simply because it contains expensive buildings and busy commercial districts. It should also allow people to walk, meet, learn, rest, play, and participate in public life.

The danger is not that we have economies. It is that we increasingly allow economic thinking to decide what deserves to exist.

We have become excellent at asking, "What can we get out of this?" We ask far less often, "What is worth creating, even if we are not the ones who will benefit most from it?"

## The Education of Getting Ahead

Education is perhaps one of the clearest places to see this tension.

In Kenya, many families understand education as one of the most reliable routes to a better life. Parents sacrifice to pay school fees, students work hard to achieve good grades, and families hope that qualifications will open doors to employment and financial security.

These hopes are not misplaced. In a society where economic opportunities can be limited and competition for employment is intense, education can make an enormous difference.

But somewhere in this understandable pursuit of opportunity, we risk losing sight of the deeper purpose of learning.

A student can spend years in school and emerge with certificates without developing a lasting curiosity about the world. A person can memorise information long enough to pass an examination and forget it shortly afterwards. A graduate can become qualified for a profession without ever developing the habit of questioning assumptions, examining evidence, or pursuing a difficult idea simply because it is worth understanding.

We may produce people who know how to pass tests but have never been encouraged to ask what knowledge is for.

The problem is not that Kenyan students lack intelligence or ambition. Nor is it fair to blame teachers, many of whom work under difficult conditions. The problem is that when the stakes attached to examinations and qualifications become overwhelming, students, parents, and schools can be pushed towards prioritising measurable results over deeper understanding.

The examination becomes the destination rather than one stage in the journey.

A student may learn a scientific formula without having the opportunity to investigate a problem in the surrounding community. Another may study agriculture without being encouraged to experiment with practical ways of improving food production. A learner may write essays about society without being given enough opportunities to conduct research, debate ideas, or develop an independent argument.

These examples are not a description of every Kenyan classroom. They illustrate what education risks becoming when pressure to demonstrate results crowds out the time and resources needed for exploration.

Kenya's shift towards the Competency-Based Curriculum and competency-based assessment is an attempt to address some of these limitations. The intended emphasis includes critical thinking, creativity, practical skills, and the development of individual potential. However, policy intentions alone cannot transform learning. Teachers need adequate preparation and resources, learners need opportunities to practise skills, and assessment must genuinely reward understanding rather than simply reproduce the old habits under a new name.

A curriculum can speak about creativity while a classroom remains dominated by memorisation. It can encourage problem-solving while teachers lack the materials or time needed for meaningful projects.

The challenge is not simply to change what appears in the syllabus. It is to change our understanding of what it means to become educated.

Imagine a Kenyan student studying water scarcity. Instead of only memorising definitions, the student investigates the causes of water shortages in the local area, studies rainfall and water use, interviews community members, and proposes a practical solution.

Imagine another student studying literature and being encouraged to write a story about the community, its history, its struggles, and its aspirations. Or a student learning programming who develops a simple tool that helps a school organise information more effectively.

In each case, knowledge becomes something the student can use to understand and improve the world.

This is not an argument against examinations. Assessment has a legitimate role in education. It is an argument against allowing examinations to become the entire meaning of education.

We should want students to obtain qualifications and find employment. We should also want them to become people who can think independently, appreciate beauty, understand history, question injustice, and continue learning long after formal schooling has ended.

Education should prepare us to participate in the economy. It should also prepare us to participate in life.



## Our Cities Reveal What We Value

The question of what we value is not confined to schools or libraries. It can also be seen in the spaces we build around ourselves.

Consider Nairobi. It is a city of ambition, creativity, enterprise, and rapid change. It is also a place where questions of public space, transport, accessibility, housing, and urban design affect everyday life.

A city is more than a collection of buildings. It is an environment that shapes how people experience their time. It determines whether a child has somewhere safe to play, whether a pedestrian can move comfortably, whether neighbours have somewhere to gather, and whether public spaces are accessible to people who cannot afford to pay for private entertainment.

The design of a city expresses a philosophy, even when nobody explicitly states one.

If we build places primarily for vehicles, commercial activity, and private consumption, we create one kind of urban life. If we also invest in parks, libraries, walkable streets, public art, community facilities, and buildings that respect the local environment, we create something different.

Neither beauty nor public space is a luxury that only wealthy societies can afford. Thoughtful design can improve safety, health, dignity, and social connection. It can also help cities respond to environmental challenges.

Community-led public-space projects in places such as Kibera demonstrate that neighbourhood improvements can combine practical infrastructure with spaces for play, gathering, and local economic activity. Such projects remind us that building for the future does not always require a monumental budget. It can begin with listening to residents and creating something that serves several human needs at once.

This is an important distinction. Long-term thinking is not simply about building structures that look impressive or last for centuries. It is about building things that continue to serve people well.

A modest community library that remains useful for generations may be a greater achievement than an expensive building that looks impressive but fails to serve the public.

Perhaps we should ask a different question when evaluating development: not only how much has been constructed, but how much human life has been improved by what we have constructed.

## We Have Confused Speed With Progress

Technology is not the enemy of patience. In fact, it can make patience more productive. Modern engineering, digital tools, scientific instruments, and artificial intelligence can help us solve problems that would otherwise consume enormous amounts of time and effort.

The question is what we choose to do with the time and power technology gives us.

If every improvement in efficiency simply creates pressure to produce even more, we may never experience the freedom that efficiency was supposed to provide. We finish one task only to receive three more. We automate a process and immediately increase the expected output. We gain tools that make communication easier but find ourselves more overwhelmed by messages.

We become faster without necessarily becoming wiser.

The same contradiction appears in the physical world. We can construct buildings using sophisticated engineering and advanced materials, yet many projects are designed under intense pressure to reduce costs and maximise returns. We can manufacture products in enormous quantities, but durability and repairability may receive less attention than price and convenience.

This does not mean modern architecture or manufacturing is inherently inferior. Contemporary engineers and designers create extraordinary work, and older structures were not all masterpieces. The point is that our technological capacity does not guarantee that we will use it to create a more beautiful or thoughtful world.

We need to distinguish between the ability to make something and the wisdom to decide what should be made.

A civilisation can be technologically advanced and still be intellectually or culturally impoverished. It can produce more goods without producing a greater sense of meaning. It can generate enormous wealth while neglecting the public spaces, institutions, relationships, and traditions that help people feel connected to one another.

Progress should not be measured only by how much we can produce or how quickly we can produce it. It should also be measured by the quality of the lives we create around that production.

## What Will We Leave Behind?

Imagine someone living three hundred years from now looking back at our time.

What will they find?

They will almost certainly discover extraordinary scientific achievements, digital records, advanced machines, and evidence of a civilisation that could communicate instantly across the planet. They may marvel at our medical discoveries, space exploration, engineering, and computing.

But will they also find evidence that we cared about the quality of human life? Will they find public institutions built to serve generations, educational traditions that encouraged independent thought, beautiful spaces accessible to ordinary people, and works of art created with care rather than solely for attention?

Will they find that we invested in the future even when the rewards were too distant to benefit us personally?

We cannot know exactly what future generations will value. Nor should we assume that everything must last physically for centuries to be meaningful. A temporary shelter can save a life. A small conversation can change someone's direction. A short-lived work of art can matter deeply.

Still, the question of inheritance deserves our attention.

We often speak about leaving our children money, property, or opportunities. But we can also leave them institutions, knowledge, public spaces, traditions of craftsmanship, and a culture that values thoughtfulness over mere consumption.

We can leave them a world that is easier to understand, more beautiful to inhabit, and more capable of supporting human flourishing.

Or we can leave them a world in which almost everything has been optimised for the present, with little consideration for the people who must live in the future.

## Recovering the Art of Living

Perhaps mastering the art of living does not require us to abandon modern technology or romanticise earlier centuries. It requires us to reconsider our priorities.

It means recognising that some things deserve time even when they cannot be monetised. It means building schools that cultivate curiosity alongside competence. It means supporting art, libraries, research, public architecture, and scientific inquiry not only when they produce immediate returns but because they enrich civilisation.

It means creating things that are designed to endure, while remembering that endurance can also be intellectual, cultural, and moral.

It means allowing children to explore questions without immediately demanding that every interest become a career. It means reading a book without turning the experience into a productivity exercise. It means learning a craft, planting a tree, preserving a language, restoring a building, or developing an idea whose greatest contribution may be realised by someone else.

It also means looking at our own African societies not merely as places that must catch up with somebody else's version of modernity, but as places capable of contributing original ideas about how humanity should live. Our histories, languages, artistic traditions, environmental knowledge, and communal experiences can all inform the future we choose to build.

We do not need to recreate the past. We need to recover the seriousness with which people can approach the future.

Most importantly, we must recover the ability to think beyond ourselves.

The builders of a great cathedral did not necessarily know what the finished structure would mean to people centuries later. Michelangelo could not have known the full future of *David*. A scholar who preserves a text cannot predict every mind it will influence. Their work becomes part of a larger human story precisely because it exceeds the limits of their own lifetimes.

We need more of that spirit.

Not necessarily more monuments or grand buildings, but more commitment to creating things that matter beyond the moment of their creation. More institutions that preserve knowledge. More education that teaches people to think. More technology that gives human beings greater freedom rather than simply demanding greater output.

We should still seek prosperity, improve our tools, build businesses, and solve practical problems. But we must not allow the pursuit of economic success to become the only story we can tell about human existence.

A good life cannot be reduced to a salary, a collection of possessions, or a list of completed tasks. A good civilisation cannot be judged solely by its productivity.

Perhaps the real question is not whether we are more advanced than the people who came before us. In many technical respects, we unquestionably are.

The question is whether we are using that advancement to become more thoughtful, more creative, more humane, and more capable of imagining a future beyond our immediate interests.

We have learned to build faster. We have learned to communicate faster. We have learned to produce and consume at extraordinary speeds.

Now we must learn how to choose what is worth building, what is worth learning, and what is worth preserving.

Because the ultimate measure of a civilisation may not be how much it accomplishes in a single lifetime, but how thoughtfully it prepares the world for lives that will come long after its own.
