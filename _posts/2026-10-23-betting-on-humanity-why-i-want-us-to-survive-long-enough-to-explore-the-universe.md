There are nights when I find myself thinking about the universe, wondering whether somewhere beyond the limits of our observation, other beings are looking at their own skies and asking questions similar to ours.

Are we alone? Are there other civilizations? Could life exist in places we cannot yet reach, perhaps even in realities we have not learned how to understand?

I find these questions fascinating because they sit at the intersection of imagination, science, and existence itself. The universe is so vast, and our understanding of it is so limited, that it seems almost unreasonable to assume we already know everything worth knowing.

Sometimes, I wonder about other beings in other universes. I imagine civilizations that might have developed differently from us, discovered technologies we cannot yet conceive, or faced questions about existence that resemble our own. I know that imagining these possibilities does not make them true. Yet I keep returning to a question: **Why are human beings capable of imagining realities so far beyond their immediate experience?**

## Does the ability to imagine something mean it is real?

One of the most interesting things about the human mind is its ability to think beyond what it can directly observe. We can imagine distant galaxies, intelligent life on other worlds, dimensions beyond our understanding, and futures in which humanity travels between stars.

But does the fact that we can imagine something mean that it exists?

Not necessarily. Human beings can imagine dragons, immortal beings, and countless worlds that may have no counterpart in reality. Imagination alone cannot establish the existence of something. Still, the ability to imagine a possibility can give us a reason to investigate it.

The French philosopher René Descartes famously wrote, *“I think, therefore I am.”* His argument, developed in his *Meditations on First Philosophy* and *Discourse on the Method*, was concerned with the certainty of his own existence as a thinking being. He was not claiming that everything he could think about must exist outside his mind. Yet his insight raises a fascinating distinction: the existence of a thought is one thing; the existence of what that thought describes is another.

I find that distinction important. When I think about other civilizations, I know that my thoughts are real experiences occurring in my mind. Whether the civilizations themselves exist is a separate question, one that requires evidence.

Plato, centuries before Descartes, explored the difference between appearances and deeper reality through his philosophical work, including the famous Allegory of the Cave in *The Republic*. His allegory asks us to consider whether what we perceive is the whole of reality or only a limited representation of it. It does not prove that parallel universes exist, but it challenges us to remain humble about the limits of our perspective.

And then there is the philosopher Immanuel Kant, who examined the relationship between the human mind and the world we experience. His work reminds us that human knowledge is shaped by the ways our minds perceive and organize experience. We should therefore be careful about assuming that reality must fit comfortably within the boundaries of our present understanding.

These philosophers approached different questions, but their work encourages a kind of intellectual humility. We should neither accept every possibility simply because we can imagine it nor dismiss every possibility simply because we cannot yet understand it.

Perhaps the better question is not, “If I can imagine it, must it be real?” but rather, **“What would it take to discover whether it is real?”**

That question turns wonder into inquiry.

## Wonder is not enough

I could spend countless nights imagining other civilizations, wondering whether they are more advanced than us, and speculating about the places they might inhabit. I could build entire worlds in my mind without ever leaving my room.

But imagination alone will not carry humanity to another star.

If our species is ever to explore the wider universe, we will need more than curiosity. We will need science, engineering, mathematics, medicine, sustainable energy, resilient societies, and technologies that allow us to survive environments for which human beings were never naturally designed.

We will need to learn how to protect ourselves from dangers that could threaten civilization, how to use resources responsibly, and how to build systems that remain functional even when conditions become difficult. We will need to improve our ability to cooperate, solve problems, and preserve knowledge across generations.

The dream of exploring the universe is therefore connected to the practical work we do today.

Every useful scientific discovery, every reliable system we build, every improvement in education, every advance in energy technology, and every effort to make human civilization more resilient could contribute to a future we may never personally experience.

This is part of what makes building things meaningful to me. I am interested not only in what technology can do for us now, but also in what it might make possible for those who come after us.

A tool that solves a small problem today may become part of a larger chain of progress. A scientific breakthrough can create opportunities that its original discoverers never anticipated. Knowledge accumulates, technologies improve, and ideas that once belonged to science fiction sometimes become engineering challenges.

We should not assume that progress is inevitable. It requires deliberate effort, responsible decisions, and people willing to work on problems whose rewards may be distant.

## Building for a future we may never see

I know that I may not live long enough to witness humanity travel to other star systems, encounter extraterrestrial life, or develop the ability to explore whatever lies beyond the universe we currently understand.

Perhaps those milestones are centuries away. Perhaps they are much farther away than that. We do not know.

But I do not think that uncertainty makes the goal meaningless.

Most of us benefit from the work of people who lived before us. We use knowledge they developed, infrastructure they built, and technologies that emerged from generations of experimentation. Many of those people never saw the full results of their efforts.

Human progress is often a relay rather than a personal victory. One generation discovers something, another improves it, and a later generation uses it to accomplish what once seemed impossible.

I want to contribute to that relay in whatever way I can.

For me, that means taking an interest in technology and building practical solutions rather than remaining only a spectator of the future. It means learning, experimenting, creating useful tools, and developing the skills to solve real problems. It also means recognizing that the most valuable innovations are not necessarily the most spectacular ones. Sometimes, making a system more reliable, helping people access information, or making knowledge easier to use is part of the same larger human effort to improve our circumstances.

Of course, no single project can guarantee the survival of our species. Building technology also brings responsibilities. Progress that ignores safety, inequality, environmental limits, or the needs of people alive today can create new problems while attempting to solve old ones.

If humanity is to become a spacefaring civilization, we must learn not only how to reach farther, but also how to act wisely with the capabilities we develop.

Our future depends on both what we can build and the choices we make about how to use it.

## Betting on humanity

When I think about the universe at night, I am reminded of how small an individual life can seem against the scale of cosmic time. I may never know whether another civilization exists. I may never see a human being set foot on a planet orbiting another star. I may never discover whether some of the questions that occupy my imagination have answers.

Still, I find something hopeful in the fact that we can ask these questions at all.

We are a species that emerged on one planet and gradually learned to study the stars. We developed language, mathematics, scientific instruments, and technologies that allow us to investigate things far beyond our natural senses. We have made mistakes, faced enormous challenges, and repeatedly discovered that our understanding was incomplete.

None of this guarantees that humanity will survive indefinitely. We could fail. We could destroy opportunities through conflict, neglect, or short-sighted decisions. Our future is not promised to us.

But it is not entirely outside our influence, either.

We can invest in knowledge. We can make our societies more resilient. We can build technologies that address real human needs. We can protect the planet that sustains us while developing the capabilities that might one day allow us to travel beyond it. We can teach future generations to question, explore, cooperate, and think beyond the limits of their own lifetimes.

I do not need certainty that humanity will reach the stars before I begin working toward a better future. I only need to believe that the effort is worth making.

Perhaps somewhere, on a distant world, another intelligent species is looking up at its night sky and wondering whether it is alone. Perhaps there are no such beings. At present, we do not have a confirmed answer.

What we do have is a remarkable opportunity: the opportunity to learn more about the universe and to help ensure that humanity remains capable of asking questions about it.

I know I may not live to witness the final outcome. That is why I want to contribute while I can.

**My goal is not simply to imagine a future in which humanity explores the universe. It is to help build the conditions that give humanity a better chance of reaching that future.**

I am betting on humanity—not because I know we will win, but because I believe the possibility is worth working for.
