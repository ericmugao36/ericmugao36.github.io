Some critics of Jesus ask why He never explicitly condemned slavery. He never owned a slave, but why didn't He stand before the crowds and denounce the institution by name? Others wonder why, when asked about paying taxes to Rome, He simply answered, "Give to Caesar what belongs to Caesar." Wasn't that the perfect opportunity to condemn Roman occupation?

Perhaps the problem lies in what people expected Jesus to be. Many wanted a political liberator, a powerful descendant of David who would overthrow Rome and restore Israel by force. They wanted a Messiah who would defeat their enemies. When Jesus refused to play that role, many concluded that He had failed to deliver what they needed.

But Jesus was addressing something much deeper than the political struggles of His time. He was confronting the human heart, the very thing that makes oppression, slavery, exploitation and violence possible in the first place.

Consider His teachings in the Sermon on the Mount. He took the commandment against murder and went beyond the physical act, addressing the anger and hatred that precede it. He took the prohibition against adultery and challenged the lustful intentions of the heart. When people invoked the principle of an eye for an eye, He taught them to turn the other cheek, love their enemies and pray for those who persecute them. Even His instruction to walk the second mile spoke directly to people living under Roman occupation, where soldiers could compel them to carry their loads.

Notice the pattern. Jesus was not simply telling people which actions were wrong. He was confronting the attitudes, desires and impulses that produce those actions. He wanted to change the person behind the behaviour. 

Walking the extra mile meant choosing to keep going on your own free will. It caught the oppressor off guard, striping them of their power to provoke hatred, and transforming a humiliating demand into an act of voluntary service

This is important because humanity has always found ways to justify its cruelty. The slaveholder sees himself as superior, the occupier sees his victims as inferior, and the powerful convince themselves that their interests matter more than the lives of others. Meanwhile, everyone finds someone else to blame. Jesus challenged this instinct by refusing to let people escape moral examination simply because they could identify a worse offender.

This does not mean slavery, genocide or oppression are any less evil. It means that condemning these things is not enough if the human attitudes that produce them remain untouched. A person can condemn an atrocity committed by someone else while harbouring the same arrogance, hatred and appetite for power that made it possible.

That is why Jesus' command to love one's enemies was so radical. He was attempting to break the cycle in which every group sees itself as righteous and its enemies as deserving of suffering. His message demanded something far more difficult than defeating an opponent: overcoming the evil within oneself.

Imagine a world in which people genuinely practised these teachings. Where greed gave way to generosity, hatred to forgiveness, lust to self-control, pride to humility, and the desire to dominate others to genuine love. Slavery would have no moral foundation, exploitation would lose its justification, and persecution would find no willing participants. Many atrocities would become unthinkable before anyone needed to condemn them.

Perhaps that is what we sometimes miss about Jesus. He was not merely offering solutions to the injustices of one generation. He was confronting the human condition that allows those injustices to emerge in every generation. Remove one empire and another can rise. Abolish one institution and people can invent another means of exploiting their neighbours.

Jesus went after the root, not merely the symptoms. His ambition was not simply to change who held power, but to change what people did with power. And that transformation, beginning with the individual human heart, was far more demanding than overthrowing Rome.
