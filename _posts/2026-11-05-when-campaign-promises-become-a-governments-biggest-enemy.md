title: When Campaign Promises Become a Government's Biggest Enemy
date: 2026-11-05T04:06:00.000+03:00

---
There is a dangerous thing about politics that every government should understand: you can deceive people about their problems for years, but once you help them recognize those problems, you cannot easily convince them to ignore them again.

Donald Trump understands this now, William Ruto will understand it too.

Both men built their political campaigns around a similar idea: the ordinary citizen had been abandoned by an established political class, and they were the people who would finally do something about it.

They presented themselves as outsiders. They promised to challenge the establishment, confront powerful interests and put ordinary citizens first. They understood the frustrations of people who felt that the political system was working for everyone except them.

And that is precisely why their political stories are worth comparing.

The American midterm elections of November 2026 have delivered a particularly important lesson. Democrats have taken control of both the House of Representatives and the Senate, dealing a major political blow to Trump's Republican Party. Some of the states that had overwhelmingly supported Trump in 2024 have now turned against his party.

What happened?

Did Americans suddenly fall in love with the Democrats? Did they forget their disagreements with the previous Democratic administration? Did their political ideologies change overnight?

Probably not.

What happened is that many voters became disappointed with the people they had trusted to deliver change.

And perhaps that is the biggest lesson for Kenya as we approach the 2027 General Election.

## When Trump promised to put Americans first

When Donald Trump returned to the campaign trail in 2024, he understood the frustrations of ordinary Americans.

He promised to bring down the cost of living, secure the borders, end America's involvement in endless foreign wars and put American interests first. He also promised transparency around the Epstein files, an issue that had attracted considerable public attention.

His message was simple: America had been taken in the wrong direction by an establishment that cared more about its interests than those of ordinary citizens.

He was not merely selling policies. He was selling a feeling. He was telling Americans that their frustrations were legitimate and that somebody finally understood them.

His message found an audience beyond traditional Republican voters. He attracted support from sections of the younger electorate, independent voters and communities that had previously leaned towards Democrats. He also won all seven battleground states in the 2024 presidential election.

For many of his supporters, Trump represented something bigger than a presidential candidate. He was the man who would dismantle a system they believed had failed them.

But winning an election on promises is one thing. Governing according to those promises is another.

Two years later, the American political environment looks very different.

The United States became involved in a major military conflict with Iran in February 2026. The conflict, which continued despite attempts at a ceasefire, brought enormous economic and political consequences. Rising fuel prices became another source of frustration for households already struggling with the cost of living.

The administration's handling of the Epstein files also became a source of controversy among people who had expected transparency.

The America First message began facing a difficult question: if America must come first, why are ordinary Americans being asked to bear the economic costs of another foreign conflict?

Some Republican voices defended the economic consequences of confronting Iran, arguing that the cost of fuel was a price worth paying to prevent Iran from obtaining nuclear weapons. But that is precisely the kind of argument that becomes difficult to sell to a family struggling to pay its bills.

The voters who wanted lower living costs were now being asked to accept higher costs in the name of a foreign policy objective.

And the voters who wanted an end to endless wars were watching another conflict unfold.

The contradiction between the campaign message and the governing reality became increasingly difficult to ignore.

The result was a devastating midterm defeat for Republicans.

Democrats did not necessarily win because every American suddenly agreed with their ideology. They benefited because voters had an opportunity to express their dissatisfaction with the party in power.

That distinction matters.

Sometimes voters are not voting because they have fallen in love with the alternative. They are voting because they have lost confidence in the people they previously trusted.

## The Kenyan version of the same political story

Now let us come home.

In 2022, William Ruto built a political movement around the ordinary Kenyan.

He positioned himself as the champion of the hustler, the person who understood the struggles of someone trying to earn a living from a small business, a roadside stall or an informal job.

His campaign presented a sharp contrast between hustlers and dynasties.

On one side were ordinary citizens struggling to make ends meet. On the other were political families that had dominated the country's leadership for generations.

Ruto questioned why the same political families should continue determining Kenya's future. He presented himself as someone who had risen from humble beginnings, including his experience selling chicken, and understood the challenges facing ordinary people.

His political message was powerful because it touched on something real.

Kenyans were tired of political families, economic inequality, corruption, unemployment and a system that seemed to reward political connections more than hard work.

The dynasty argument was particularly interesting.

Raila Odinga was the son of Jaramogi Oginga Odinga, Kenya's first Vice-President after independence. Uhuru Kenyatta was the son of Jomo Kenyatta, Kenya's first President.

Ruto used this political history to present himself as an alternative to an establishment dominated by familiar political names.

He was telling Kenyans that the presidency did not have to remain the preserve of political dynasties.

And many ordinary citizens connected with that message.

But here is where the problem begins.

What happens when the person who promised to fight the establishment becomes the person citizens believe they need to hold accountable for the same problems?

What happens when the hustler who promised economic relief presides over a government that citizens continue to associate with a high cost of living, increased financial pressure and economic uncertainty?

What happens when a government that promised to understand ordinary citizens finds itself facing young people protesting against its policies, only for the protests to be met with deadly force?

The Gen Z protests of 2024 exposed a serious disconnect between the government and a generation that was demanding accountability, better governance and an end to corruption

For many young Kenyans, the question was no longer simply about taxation. It was about the kind of country they wanted to live in and the kind of government they were willing to accept.

The same government that had campaigned on understanding the ordinary citizen was now being challenged by citizens who felt unheard.

And that is the danger of political promises.

You cannot spend an entire campaign telling people that you understand their suffering, only to become dismissive when they demand solutions.

You cannot build a political identity around fighting the establishment and then expect citizens never to question whether you have become part of the establishment yourself.

The question is no longer whether Ruto understands the hustler. The question is whether his government has delivered the future it promised that hustler.

## You cannot awaken citizens and expect them to go back to sleep

There is one similarity between Trump and Ruto that I find particularly interesting.

Both men helped ordinary citizens recognize and articulate their frustrations.

Trump gave many Americans a language through which to express their anger towards Washington, the political establishment and the economic system.

Ruto gave many Kenyans a language through which to question political dynasties, economic inequality and the concentration of political power among familiar names.

In their own ways, both men helped bring certain political conversations into the mainstream.

But there is a consequence to doing this.

Once you teach people to question the establishment, they eventually begin questioning you.

Once you tell people that political families have monopolized monopolised power, they will eventually ask whether your own leadership is any different.

Once you tell people that politicians have failed them, they will eventually measure your government against that same standard.

You cannot tell citizens to stop accepting excuses from previous governments and then expect them to accept excuses from yours.

You cannot tell people that they deserve better and then become uncomfortable when they demand better from you.

This is perhaps the most important political lesson from the American midterms.

Trump may have defeated the establishment in 2024, but the voters who helped him win have not surrendered their right to hold him accountable.

And in Kenya, the people who voted for Ruto in 2022 have not surrendered their right to question his government.

A vote is not a lifetime contract.

## The mainstream media is no longer the gatekeeper

There is another lesson that Kenyan politicians should pay attention to.

We are no longer living in an era when a handful of television stations, newspapers and radio presenters could determine the political conversation.

Trump still enjoys considerable support from sections of mainstream conservative media, including Fox News. However, the broader media ecosystem that helped build his political movement is much bigger than traditional television.

People such as Tucker Carlson, Candace Owens and Joe Rogan have developed influential platforms outside the conventional mainstream media structure. Their audiences consume political conversations through podcasts, independent channels, social media and online communities.

These platforms are not necessarily politically neutral, and their hosts do not always agree with one another. But they demonstrate something important: political influence is no longer concentrated in traditional newsrooms.

The same transformation is happening in Kenya.

A young Kenyan can wake up, watch a parliamentary debate, examine a government budget, listen to an independent political commentator, compare economic figures and discuss the country's affairs with thousands of other citizens without switching on Citizen TV or KBC.

A video recorded on a mobile phone can reach millions of people before a traditional newsroom decides whether it deserves coverage.

A government can control a press conference, but it cannot easily control every conversation happening online.

This does not mean that everything circulating on social media is true. Misinformation, propaganda and political manipulation are real problems. Citizens must learn to verify information rather than believe everything they encounter.

But politicians should understand that the information environment has fundamentally changed.

You can no longer assume that controlling the traditional media narrative means controlling what citizens believe.

The younger generation is particularly important in this transformation. Many young people are not waiting for political parties to explain what is happening. They are searching for information, comparing competing arguments and forming their own conclusions.

The Gen Z movement demonstrated that political mobilisation does not always need to begin in party headquarters or with instructions from established political leaders.

It can begin with a conversation online.

And that should worry any politician who believes that tribal arithmetic and traditional campaign machinery are enough to guarantee victory.

## The 2027 election should be about what happens after the promises

Kenya is heading towards another important electoral moment in 2027.

The temptation will be to turn the election into another contest of personalities, ethnic numbers and political survival.

But perhaps citizens should approach it differently.

We should begin by asking every political hopeful some simple questions.

What exactly will you do about the cost of living?

How will you create employment opportunities?

How will you address corruption and public expenditure?

What is your plan for public debt?

How will you protect the rights of citizens who disagree with your government?

How will we measure your performance after one year, two years and five years?

And, most importantly, what happens if you fail?

These questions should not be reserved only for Ruto. They should be directed at everyone seeking public office.

We should stop treating political manifestos as campaign decorations. They should become accountability documents against which governments are measured.

A leader's responsibility is not to reward the ethnic group that helped elect them. It is to serve the entire country.

The next election should not simply be about finding another person who promises to rescue us.

It should be about finding people with credible plans, measurable commitments and a willingness to be held accountable.

**You can open people's eyes by telling them the truth about the problems they face. But once their eyes are open, you must be prepared to live up to the promises you made.**

Trump is learning that lesson in America.

Ruto and the political class in Kenya should pay attention.
