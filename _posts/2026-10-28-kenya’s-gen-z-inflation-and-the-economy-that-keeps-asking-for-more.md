*Why are young Kenyans paying more to survive while struggling to find the jobs and opportunities that would enable them to thrive?*

A young Kenyan wakes up, checks their phone and finds another discussion about the cost of living. Someone complains about rent. Another talks about the price of food. Someone else has sent out dozens of job applications without receiving a response. In the comments, the same question keeps returning in different forms, how are we supposed to build a future in this economy?

This frustration found its way into the streets in June 2024, when thousands of young Kenyans demonstrated against the Finance Bill. What began as resistance to proposed tax increases quickly became a broader demand for accountability, economic opportunity and a government that understood the struggles of ordinary citizens. The message was bigger than a tax bill. It was about the relationship between what Kenyans contribute and what they receive in return.

To understand why that relationship matters, we must first understand inflation.

Inflation is the general rise in prices over time. Imagine earning KSh 20,000 a month and spending KSh 12,000 on food, transport and other necessities. If those expenses rise while your salary remains unchanged, you have less money available for rent, savings, education or starting a business. Your salary has not fallen on paper, but your purchasing power has.

This is the quiet punishment of inflation, you can work just as hard and still afford less.

Kenya’s official statistics help illustrate the pressure. According to the Kenya National Bureau of Statistics, annual inflation reached 6.8 per cent in September 2026, driven partly by food prices, which rose by 9.5 per cent, and transport, which rose by 15.6 per cent over the preceding year. The national inflation rate is an average, so individual households may experience a different increase depending on what they spend their money on. Nevertheless, when necessities become more expensive, the pressure falls hardest on people whose incomes leave little room for adjustment. 

And here is the catch: when inflation falls, prices do not necessarily fall with it. It usually means prices are increasing more slowly. If unga, rent and transport have already become expensive, a lower inflation rate does not automatically restore yesterday’s affordability.

For someone with a secure, well-paying job, this may be an inconvenience. For a young graduate without employment, a casual worker whose income changes from week to week, or a small business owner struggling to find customers, it can be the difference between moving forward and remaining trapped.

The World Bank has highlighted the scale of Kenya’s youth employment challenge, noting that many young people work in informal jobs with limited security and opportunities for advancement. Economic growth alone does not guarantee that young people will find stable, productive employment.

This is where inflation meets unemployment. A country can produce impressive economic growth figures while many of its young people remain unable to find decent work. Businesses may be operating, money may be changing hands, and buildings may be going up, yet the benefits can remain unevenly distributed. For a young Kenyan, the economy is not an abstract collection of statistics. It is the job that never comes, the business that cannot get affordable credit, and the salary that disappears before the month ends.

Government spending is central to this story. The government is a major buyer of goods and services, an employer, a borrower and an investor. Its spending can create jobs and expand the economy’s productive capacity. A well-built road can reduce transport costs. Reliable electricity can help a manufacturer grow. Effective agricultural investment can improve food supply and reduce pressure on prices.

But public spending can also become a burden when the money borrowed or collected from taxpayers fails to produce lasting value.

Consider what happens when billions of shillings are spent on projects that are poorly planned, overpriced or abandoned. The money has left the public purse, but the promised benefits may never arrive. 

If corruption and waste divert resources away from their intended purposes, Kenyans are left with fewer useful services, fewer opportunities and, potentially, more debt to repay.

This does not mean that every government project is wasteful or that borrowing is always wrong. Productive borrowing can help a country build assets that generate benefits for decades. The problem is borrowing and spending without sufficient returns, particularly when citizens are then asked to shoulder more taxes.

Nor does every increase in government spending automatically cause inflation. The effect depends on how the spending is financed, what it purchases and whether the economy can supply the additional goods and services being demanded. But when demand rises faster than production, prices can come under pressure. When spending is financed through borrowing, it can also increase debt-service costs and compete with private businesses for available financing.

Then comes the promise of campaign handouts.

Giving money to people who are struggling can provide essential relief. A family that cannot afford food needs help now, not a lecture about productivity. Well-designed cash transfers and social protection programmes can protect vulnerable households and help people during difficult periods.

But there is a difference between helping someone survive and building an economy in which they can earn a sustainable income.

Suppose a young person receives KSh 10,000. They may spend it on food, rent, transport, debt repayments or other urgent needs. There is nothing wrong with that; these are real expenses. The money then moves to shops, landlords, suppliers, businesses and possibly lenders. Those receiving it may benefit, but the young person will be left without a reliable source of income once the money is exhausted.

The problem is not that people consume. Consumption is essential to economic activity, and businesses need customers. The problem is expecting one-off payments to solve unemployment without creating productive opportunities.

If public support is intended to change lives over the long term, it should also help people acquire skills, access markets, obtain suitable financing, buy productive equipment and establish viable businesses. Some recipients will still need direct assistance, and not every business will succeed. But a programme that builds earning capacity has the potential to deliver benefits beyond the initial payment.

Kenya therefore needs to ask a more demanding question about every shilling it spends: does this expenditure merely move money around, or does it help the country produce more value?

The answer begins with accountability. Public contracts must deliver what they promise. Corruption must carry consequences. Borrowing should be judged against realistic economic returns. Essential services should receive effective funding, while unnecessary expenditure should face scrutiny. 

Support for young people should be connected, where appropriate, to practical training, affordable financing, infrastructure and access to customers.

The private sector must also have room to grow. Small businesses need reliable electricity, predictable rules, fair competition and access to capital on terms they can sustain. Agriculture needs better productivity and stronger routes to market. 

Young people need more than advice to become entrepreneurs; they need an environment in which starting and growing a business is possible.

The demand should not be simply for lower prices, higher salaries or free money. It is for an economy in which effort has a reasonable chance of producing a better life.

That is the connection between inflation, unemployment and government accountability. 

When prices rise faster than incomes, when jobs remain scarce, and when public resources fail to create enough lasting value, frustration becomes inevitable. Demanding accountability is therefore not separate from demanding economic opportunity. It is part of the same argument.

Kenya cannot promise that prices will never rise. Nor can any government guarantee every young person a job. But it can make choices that expand production, encourage investment, protect vulnerable citizens and ensure that public money delivers measurable benefits.

Ultimately, the test of an economy is not simply how much money passes through it, how many projects are announced or how much the government distributes. It is whether ordinary people can earn a living, afford their basic needs and build something that lasts.

**The question Kenya’s Gen Z has placed before the country is simple: if everyone is being asked to contribute, where is the value being created?**

Until that question receives a convincing answer, the debate about the cost of living will remain about far more than the price of unga. It will be about the future of an entire generation.
