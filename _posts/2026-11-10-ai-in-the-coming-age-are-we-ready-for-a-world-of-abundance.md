Imagine waking up one morning in a world where food is affordable, building a house takes days rather than months, medical services are accessible to everyone, and machines handle much of the labour that currently consumes our time. Imagine having a personal assistant who can research, organise your business, teach your children and help you solve problems that would otherwise require several professionals.

This might sound like science fiction, but the technology that could make it possible is already developing. Artificial intelligence (AI), robotics and automation are advancing at a pace that should make every government, business owner and ordinary citizen pay attention.

**We could be approaching a golden age of human civilisation.**

### The signs are already here

In July 2025, researchers at Johns Hopkins University announced a remarkable breakthrough in surgical robotics. A robot trained using videos of surgical procedures successfully performed a complex stage of gallbladder removal on a lifelike patient without human assistance. It could also respond to voice instructions and adapt to unexpected situations. This was an experimental demonstration, not an operation on a human patient, but it showed just how far surgical automation could go.

In September 2025, another development demonstrated AI-guided surgical technology assisting a surgeon during an actual operation in Chile. The system independently adjusted a surgical camera, allowing the surgeon to concentrate on the procedure without needing another person to operate the camera.

These developments are important because they show that AI is moving beyond answering questions and generating pictures. It is beginning to interact with the physical world, assist professionals and undertake complex tasks.

We are also witnessing the emergence of AI agents: systems capable of planning, researching, using software, executing instructions and completing multi-step assignments with limited human supervision. Their capabilities will continue improving.

### A billion robots in ten years?

In September 2026, Elon Musk made a prediction that deserves serious attention. Speaking at the G20 Innovation Ministerial, he suggested that the world could have more than one billion humanoid robots within ten years, potentially producing several times as much as an average human worker.

His argument is that robots will eventually help manufacture other robots, creating a cycle in which production accelerates dramatically.

Of course, Musk is known for ambitious predictions, and we should not confuse his forecast with a guaranteed outcome. But consider the implications if even a fraction of this becomes reality.

Tesla is not the only player in this race. Chinese manufacturers, including Unitree and AgiBot, are rapidly developing humanoid robots and expanding production. Other technology companies will compete for the same market.

Competition matters because it drives innovation, improves quality and, potentially, reduces prices. As production scales up, technologies that initially cost hundreds of thousands of dollars could eventually become accessible to ordinary households and small businesses.

A robot that can construct buildings, work on farms, transport goods, clean homes or assist elderly people could fundamentally change the economics of everyday life.

### From scarcity to abundance

This is where things become particularly interesting.

Humanity has enormous productive potential, but much of it is currently limited by the cost of labour, time, equipment and resources. Building a house requires workers, materials, transport and months of coordination. Producing food requires land, machinery, labour and distribution networks.

Now imagine combining intelligent machines, automated factories, agricultural technology and cheap energy.

If we can dramatically reduce the cost of producing food, constructing houses and manufacturing essential goods, we could begin solving two of humanity's oldest problems: hunger and inadequate shelter.

This does not mean poverty will automatically disappear. Resources still have to be distributed, land ownership must be addressed, and governments must ensure that technological progress benefits more than a handful of corporations.

But imagine what becomes possible when people no longer spend most of their lives struggling to afford basic necessities. Human creativity, education, scientific research, entrepreneurship and exploration could flourish in ways we have barely imagined.

The real promise of AI is not simply that machines will do our work. It is that humanity could finally have more time and resources to pursue greater things.

### The challenge: Compute, energy and preparation

However, this future will not run on enthusiasm alone.

AI requires enormous computing capacity. Behind every intelligent system are specialised chips, servers and data centres processing vast quantities of information.

And all this requires electricity.

The International Energy Agency estimates that global data-centre electricity consumption could roughly double from around 485 terawatt-hours in 2025 to 950 terawatt-hours by 2030. AI is a major driver of this growth.

This means energy infrastructure will become one of the defining issues of the AI revolution. Countries that can provide reliable, affordable electricity, computing infrastructure and connectivity will have a significant advantage.

For Kenya, this presents both a challenge and an opportunity.

Our geothermal potential gives us an advantage in developing reliable, cleaner energy. We also have a growing technology ecosystem, established mobile connectivity and a young population that can be trained to participate in this emerging economy.

In March 2025, Kenya launched its National AI Strategy for 2025–2030, focusing on digital infrastructure, data and AI governance, and research, innovation and commercialisation. This is an important starting point, but implementation will matter more than the announcement. [Ministry of ICT, Kenya](https://ict.go.ke/kenyas-artificial-intelligence-ai-strategy-2025-2030-launched-kicc-nairobi).

We should be thinking about affordable electricity, data centres, high-speed internet, technical education, research institutions and policies that encourage local innovation. Our universities should prepare graduates for an economy where working alongside intelligent machines will become increasingly important.

Businesses, too, should start exploring how AI can improve productivity rather than waiting until the technology becomes unavoidable.

### So, how long until the golden age?

Nobody knows.

Some of the predictions being made today may prove wildly optimistic. Humanoid robots still face challenges involving cost, reliability, safety and dexterity. AI systems also make mistakes, and deploying them in critical environments requires proper oversight.

But the direction of progress is difficult to ignore.

The important question is not whether every prediction will come true by 2036. It is whether we will be prepared when these technologies become commercially accessible.

For Kenya, the danger is not simply that AI will replace certain jobs. It is that we could become consumers of technologies developed elsewhere, paying for solutions that we could have developed ourselves.

There is an opportunity here for Kenyan entrepreneurs, developers, professionals and businesses to start building AI-powered services around local problems, from agriculture and education to healthcare, logistics and financial management.

The next generation of successful businesses may not necessarily employ thousands of people. They may employ a handful of skilled people who know how to direct hundreds of intelligent agents and automated systems.

We should therefore stop treating AI as a passing technological trend. It is becoming an essential part of the world's productive infrastructure.

**A golden age is not guaranteed by technology alone. It will depend on who owns the technology, who can access it and how wisely we choose to use it.**

The future is being built now. The question is whether Kenya intends to help build it or simply wait to buy it.
